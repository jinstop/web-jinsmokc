### The comparison people actually make

Most enquiries compare two or three furnaces on purchase price.

That comparison is easy to do and it is not the useful one. Over ten years, the purchase price is often a minority of what the furnace has cost you. The items that dominate are the ones that arrive after the invoice.

Below is a structure for thinking about it. We put it together because we have watched customers get surprised by the running cost, and we would rather they compared properly before buying than discover it afterwards.

### The cost items that are easy to forget

**Energy.** This is the big one. Two furnaces with identical stated temperatures and identical throughput can differ substantially in energy consumption, because of:

- Insulation quality and how it has aged
- Excess air in combustion, a burner tuning issue
- Leakage through worn seals, especially under atmosphere control
- Standby losses on a line that idles between shifts

Energy cost per part is the number to compare across suppliers. It requires knowing gas and electricity prices in your market, which only you have.

**Throughput loss.** A line rated for 20 hours a day, running two shifts, has unused capacity. If a cheaper furnace is 15% slower and you only run one shift, its real cost per year over a decade can exceed a more expensive line that runs three shifts.

**Yield.** This is the largest item and the hardest to see.

A furnace with a uniformity class of ±10 °C instead of ±5 °C will produce parts at the edge of your specification more often. If your customer rejects them, the cost is not the part — it is the batch, the delay, the reordering and the relationship.

**Downtime.** Every hour the line is down costs more than the hour is worth. Availability is a function of component quality, seal design, and how many proprietary spares there are.

**Spare parts.** Ask for a list with lead times, not just a price. A heating element that is 8 weeks away is a 8-week production problem.

**Refractory.** A new lining is a planned cost at a known interval. An unplanned lining failure on an old furnace is an unplanned outage at whatever hour it happens.

### The comparison we would make

Instead of comparing quotations, compare **ten-year operating cost per part**:

```
Total cost = purchase
           + installation and commissioning
           + energy × hours running
           + maintenance and spares
           + planned refractory
           + downtime losses
           + scrap from yield
           ─────────────────────────────
           ÷ parts produced over ten years
```

Put every supplier's numbers into the same table, using the same assumptions. The ranking usually changes.

### Where the money actually is

From experience across a number of installations, the general pattern is:

- **Energy** is the largest recurring cost and the one most sensitive to specification quality
- **Yield** is the largest total cost and the one most sensitive to uniformity
- **Purchase price** matters, but rarely decides the outcome on its own

This is why a supplier quoting a wider working width, more control zones and a tighter uniformity class is not simply charging more. They are quoting a different operating cost.

### What to ask any supplier

1. **What is the specified uniformity class, and at what load and atmosphere is it measured?**
2. **What is the expected fuel or electricity consumption at rated throughput?** Ask for the units per hour, not the total.
3. **What is the quoted lead time on wear spares, and which are proprietary?**
4. **What is the planned refractory service interval, and what does the interval depend on?**
5. **What energy consumption did comparable installations actually measure?** Ask for references you can call.

A supplier who answers all five precisely is telling you they have thought about the operating life. A supplier who answers none of them is quoting a machine price.

### Where we are biased

We supply furnaces, so we are not neutral in this comparison, and it is worth naming that.

Our bias is toward tighter uniformity and better sealing than a customer may think they need. The counter-argument is fair: for some processes and some budgets, that is over-specification, and we would rather say so than sell it.

So the useful version of this article is not "always buy the better furnace". It is **"compare properly before you buy, on costs that will actually appear"**. If you do that and the cheaper machine still wins, take the cheaper machine — that is a real result, not a compromise.
