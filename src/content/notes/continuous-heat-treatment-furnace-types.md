### What "continuous" actually means

A continuous furnace does not mean the part moves through the furnace quickly. It means the process never stops.

In a batch furnace, a basket of parts goes in, the basket waits through the whole cycle, and comes out. Between cycles the furnace is doing nothing but heating and holding.

In a continuous furnace, parts enter at one end and leave at the other while heating, holding and cooling happen simultaneously in different zones. The furnace never idles thermally.

That single difference drives three things customers care about:

- **Throughput.** Continuous capacity is an hourly figure, not a cycle figure.
- **Consistency.** Every part sees the same thermal history, because every part travels the same path at the same rate.
- **Yield.** Less thermal cycling of the chamber means less refractory wear over time.

### The four platforms

**Continuous mesh belt furnace**

Parts sit on a woven metal belt driven through the heated zone. This is the workhorse for small to medium components — fasteners, small castings, machined parts, some bearings.

The belt is the limiting factor. Part weight, footprint and pitch all have to suit the belt, and the part must tolerate belt contact marks. Very small or very delicate parts that would drop through the mesh need a tray or a fixture instead.

**Roller hearth furnace**

Parts ride on powered rollers through the tunnel. Built for heavier and longer parts that a mesh belt cannot carry — shafts, rings, flanges, forgings, long bars.

Each roller is driven and, in a well-designed furnace, individually controlled to keep the load moving evenly. Sealing a roller table for atmosphere control is more demanding than sealing a belt, which is why roller hearth furnaces tend to be more expensive to build.

**Car bottom furnace**

A batch furnace that happens to run through a continuous tunnel. Parts are loaded onto rail-mounted cars, and a car shuttle moves them through heating, holding and discharge.

This gives the flexibility of batch processing inside a continuous layout. It suits operations where the part mix changes often, or where different families share one furnace.

**Pusher furnace**

The simplest mechanism. A ram pushes the load continuously from a charge end through the heated length onto a discharge conveyor at the far end.

Few moving parts, easy maintenance, and easy to integrate with upstream and downstream handling. It suits long bars and linear parts where orientation matters and the section is consistent.

### How to choose

The decision follows four questions, in this order:

1. **What is the part?** Size, weight, geometry, and whether it can sit on a belt.
2. **What is the process?** Hardening and tempering, annealing, normalising, stress relieving, solution treatment, sintering.
3. **What atmosphere?** Air, nitrogen, hydrogen, vacuum, or endothermic gas. This drives sealing and purging complexity more than any other factor.
4. **What throughput, and how many hours a day?** A line sized for 20 hours of production will disappoint a shop running two shifts.

Once those are answered, the platform follows. Most lines are one primary type plus a quench or cooling section at the discharge end.

### One common mistake

Buyers often size the furnace by the part that is easiest to measure — usually the lightest part in the range — then discover that the heavy part needs a different handling arrangement and a longer dwell.

A furnace is a system: heating, atmosphere, conveying, quench and control interact. If one of them is sized for a different part than the others, the line underperforms.

Sizing against the whole part range, including the awkward one, is what we do at the enquiry stage. It is the part of the conversation worth having before anything is quoted.
