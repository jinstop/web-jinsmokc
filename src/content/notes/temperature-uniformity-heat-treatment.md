### The number on the spec sheet

Almost every furnace quotation states a maximum temperature. That number is the least interesting thing about the furnace.

The number that determines whether your parts come out consistent is **temperature uniformity across the work zone** — the difference between the hottest and coldest point where parts actually sit.

A furnace that reaches 950 °C at one thermocouple and 938 °C at the other end is not a 950 °C furnace for your process. It is a furnace that produces two different results depending on where the part sat.

### Why the drift happens

**Loading pattern.** A furnace surveyed empty will read tighter than the same furnace running a production load. Parts shield each other, radiant paths change, and airflow inside the chamber is no longer the free-flow pattern you measured.

**Thermal mass variation.** Heavier parts lag behind lighter ones during ramp-up. Same furnace, same setpoint, different core temperature.

**Atmosphere.** Nitrogen and hydrogen have different thermal conductivity and heat capacity than air. A furnace that measures uniform in air may not be uniform in gas — and gas is usually the condition that matters.

**Door and seal leakage.** Every ingress of ambient air is a cold spot forming near the opening. In an atmosphere-controlled line this shows up as a lengthwise gradient, worst near the charge end.

**End effects.** The first and last metre of any tunnel is affected by the opening. This is normal and expected — what matters is whether it is excluded from the rated work zone, as it should be.

### How uniformity is specified

Ask three questions of any supplier:

1. **Where is the survey taken?** Across the full work zone, not at a single plane.
2. **Is it under load or unloaded?** Ideally both, and you should be told which.
3. **What atmosphere is it measured in?** Air or the process gas.

If the answers are vague, the number on the spec sheet is decoration.

Our standard class is **±5 °C** across the rated work zone, measured across the width and length, and documented at commissioning with the record issued along with the machine.

### What tighter classes cost

If your process needs tighter control — certain high-alloy steels, some stainless solution treatment, aerospace work — it changes the design:

- More control zones along the length
- More thermocouple points, and closer spacing
- Different element or burner placement to correct the gradient rather than compensate with control alone
- Longer commissioning time, because more survey points take longer to measure

This is worth knowing **before** the quotation, not after. A supplier who can deliver ±3 °C is quoting a different machine from one quoting ±5 °C, and the price difference reflects that.

### Practical advice

**Run a survey after commissioning, not only at handover.** Chamber conditions change over the first months — refractory settles, seals wear, loading patterns shift. A repeat survey six months in is cheap insurance.

**Keep the loading pattern consistent.** If the line was surveyed with a full load, running it half empty changes the result. If your production varies, specify the survey condition to match the common case.

**Measure the part, not just the air.** An infrared reading on the part surface is the only number that reflects what the part actually experienced.

### The practical takeaway

When comparing quotations, treat peak temperature as a pass/fail threshold and treat uniformity as the differentiator.

Two furnaces at the same peak temperature but different uniformity classes are not the same product. The cheaper one will produce the same scrap rate until someone notices it, and by then the cost is in rejected parts rather than in the invoice.
