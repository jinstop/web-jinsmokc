### Why atmosphere gets decided last and regretted first

Atmosphere requirements are usually the last line in an enquiry. The customer specifies temperature, throughput and footprint, then treats gas as a detail to be settled later.

It is not a detail. Atmosphere choice drives sealing, purging volume, gas flow rates, control strategy and operating cost — and it determines whether the process works at all.

A steel that oxidises slightly in air may produce an oxide scale your downstream customer rejects. That is not a furnace problem, it is a process specification problem, and it was cheap to prevent.

### The main options

**Air**

The default. Cheap, no sealing required, no gas supply.

Suitable for annealing of low-carbon steel, stress relieving, normalising, preheating — anywhere the end product tolerates surface oxidation.

Oxidation is the trade-off. Scale forms on the part, and if the customer specifies a clean surface you will need to handle that separately.

**Nitrogen**

A protective atmosphere. Parts stay bright, no scale.

Nitrogen is relatively cheap, available in most industrial regions, and inert at heat treatment temperatures.

The practical issue is **purity**. Ordinary industrial nitrogen at 99.9% can be good enough for some applications and not others; higher grades cost more. Supply pressure and flow capacity need to be checked, because a large continuous line can consume a surprising volume.

**Hydrogen**

Used for bright annealing of steel strip and for certain stainless and high-alloy work. Hydrogen actively reduces oxides, so it produces a truly clean surface.

Hydrogen is flammable and mixes explosively with air. It needs proper purging discipline, leak detection, and ventilation. This is not a decision to make on price — it is a decision to make on process requirement.

**Endothermic or ammonia dissociated gas**

Generated on site from either a carrier gas or ammonia. Used mainly for carbonitriding and carburising atmospheres.

The advantage is cost control at high volume. The trade-off is installation and maintenance complexity, plus the fact that the gas chemistry has to be kept in window or carbon content drifts.

**Vacuum**

For high-alloy and tool steels that need no scale and controlled cooling gas composition.

Vacuum is normally a **batch** process, not continuous. Vacuum furnaces are much harder to make continuous because maintaining a vacuum seal while moving parts in and out is difficult. If your process needs vacuum, expect a batch furnace or a hybrid arrangement, and expect a significantly higher price.

### Purging is where the time goes

Any atmosphere change from air to gas takes time.

The chamber has to be swept of air until the oxygen content drops to the level your process allows. That depends on chamber volume, gas flow rate, and how many leaks the seals have.

Two practical consequences:

- **Commissioning takes longer than expected.** Budget time for purging and for testing under gas, not just at temperature.
- **A leaking seal is expensive.** Continuous lines run long sealing surfaces. A leak in the first few months is a commissioning issue; the same leak after three years of production is an availability problem.

If your process runs under gas, ask what gas purity the furnace was tested at, and ask for the leak test records.

### Cost, honestly

Atmosphere choice shows up in three places:

1. **Capital** — sealing, purging manifolds, gas control skids, detectors
2. **Consumables** — nitrogen and hydrogen are metered, not free
3. **Operations** — purge time on start-up, gas monitoring, more skilled operators

The cheapest atmosphere that meets the process requirement is the right one. Going further than the process needs costs money for every hour the furnace runs; going short costs money in rejected parts.

### What to specify

When you write the enquiry, include:

- The process and the required surface condition
- Whether scale is acceptable
- Any customer specification on surface, decarburisation or pickling loss
- Gas purity and supply conditions

If you are not sure whether air is sufficient, say what the finished part looks like today and what the customer is complaining about. That is usually enough to determine the requirement, and it protects both sides from an expensive misunderstanding.
