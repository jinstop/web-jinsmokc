### The failure pattern

On most older furnaces that come to us for refurbishment, the story is the same. Output creeps down, energy use creeps up, and eventually someone opens the furnace and finds the lining is cracked, slumped or eroded.

This is almost never a single dramatic failure. It is thermal cycling, chemical attack and mechanical stress doing their work slowly, over years, in places nobody was watching.

The point worth making early: **refractory condition is visible from the outside, in advance, in most cases.** Falling fuel efficiency and uneven temperature profiles are symptoms long before the lining is critical.

### What actually causes lining damage

**Thermal cycling.** Heating and cooling the lining repeatedly drives the expansion and contraction that eventually cracks it. Continuous furnaces cycle less than batch furnaces, which is one of the practical arguments for them. Where cycling is unavoidable, the design places expansion joints correctly.

**Chemical attack.** This depends entirely on the atmosphere.

- In air, the main attack is oxidation of the lining surface
- Under nitrogen, less oxidation but any ingress of air still attacks
- Under hydrogen, water vapour forms as hydrogen reacts with oxides — **water vapour is aggressive on refractory at temperature**
- Some atmospheres carry alkali or halide contamination from the parts themselves

**Gas velocity.** High-velocity combustion air and hard-fired gas burner settings erode the lining surface. A burner that was correctly tuned for the original fuel may not be correctly tuned for a different gas supply.

**Mechanical damage.** Expansion of the work, dropped loads, tooling contact, and vibration from conveyor drive. In continuous lines, the conveyor and its supports move constantly, and a support that has crept out of alignment loads the lining asymmetrically.

**Failed seals.** Under atmosphere control, a failed seal means ambient air cycling into a hot chamber faster than designed.

### What to inspect, and when

**Weekly**

- Burner flame pattern — look for flicker, lift or impingement
- Temperature profile against the previous week; a drifting profile is the earliest warning
- Any visible cracking around door frames, charge openings and burner ports
- Quench bath level, temperature and agitation condition

**Monthly**

- Element or burner condition where visible
- Door and door seal seating, especially if running under gas
- Conveyor condition: belt tracking, roller bearings, chain tension, lubrication
- Temperature survey comparison against the commissioning record

**Annually**

- Full refractory inspection from inside, with the furnace cool
- Comparison of the uniformity survey against the commissioning baseline
- Seal integrity test
- Calibration check on all thermocouples and recording instruments
- An energy consumption review against commissioning figures

The theme is comparison. **A single reading tells you very little; a trend tells you almost everything.** We advise customers to keep the commissioning survey and compare against it annually — the drift is the diagnostic.

### On thermocouples

Thermocouples drift with time and, in some atmospheres, faster than expected.

A cheap thermocouple giving a wrong reading is worse than no reading, because the control system trusts it. Include calibration in the annual schedule, and replace elements and thermocouples as a set when you replace one.

### When refurbishment makes more sense than replacement

Refurbishment is often the right call when:

- The steelwork and frame are sound
- The heater or burner system is serviceable
- The control system can be brought up to current standards
- The required process has not changed much

It is usually worth it when the furnace is correctly sized for the current product. It is rarely worth it when the process has moved on — a furnace sized for the wrong part range will keep costing energy and yield after it has been rebuilt.

One honest note: we supply furnaces, so we are not the neutral party on this question. But the useful test is straightforward — compare the cost of refurbishment against the cost of a new line, include the downtime in both, and look at the energy consumption per part on each side of the calculation.

### When it is already too late

If the lining has failed, it has usually failed for one of the reasons above. Replacing the lining without fixing the cause produces a new lining that fails on the same schedule.

If a furnace arrives in that state, the first job is diagnosis, not bricklaying. Ask what changed — fuel supply, atmosphere, duty cycle, throughput, load pattern. Something changed, and until it is identified the rebuild is a guess.

That is the part we spend the most time on, because it is the part that determines whether the second furnace lasts longer than the first.
