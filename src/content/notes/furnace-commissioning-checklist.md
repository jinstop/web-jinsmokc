### Why commissioning matters more than it looks

Once a furnace is running production, changing anything is expensive. That makes the commissioning window the last point at which a design problem can still be corrected cheaply.

Commissioning is not a formality. It is the process of proving that the furnace does what the specification said it would do, under the conditions it will actually run in.

If the specification was right and the build was right, commissioning confirms it. If either was wrong, commissioning is where you find out — which is exactly why it should be done properly rather than rushed.

### Before the furnace arrives

Good commissioning starts before delivery. Things worth confirming:

- **Foundation and utilities are ready.** Gas or electricity capacity, compressed air, and — for atmosphere-controlled lines — the gas supply and its purity, sized for the expected flow.
- **Handling is planned.** Charge tables, unload tables, and whatever your operators will actually use. A furnace installed with no way to get parts in and out will run at a fraction of its rating.
- **The part you are processing is there.** Commissioning without production parts is commissioning an empty chamber, and the results are not comparable.

### The usual sequence

**Erection and alignment.** The furnace goes on its foundation and is checked for level and alignment. This sounds basic, and it is the step that most affects belt tracking and roller parallelism later.

**Burn-in.** The refractory lining is brought up temperature slowly, with a controlled ramp and a hold. This drives off residual moisture. Ramping too fast can damage what you have just paid for.

**Air test at temperature.** The furnace reaches setpoint in air. Flame patterns, control stability and temperature distribution are checked and corrected.

**Atmosphere commissioning.** If the line runs under gas, the furnace is purged and the uniformity survey is repeated under gas. This is where the difference between air and gas figures shows up.

**Temperature uniformity survey.** Thermocouples are placed across the work zone and a full survey is recorded. This is the document that becomes the baseline for every future comparison.

**Production trial.** The customer's actual parts, through a full cycle, with results checked. This is where loading pattern problems surface — a furnace that measures well empty and poorly loaded.

**Operator training.** Hands-on handover, not a walkthrough. The people who will run the line should have run it before we leave.

### What you should receive

**Documents**

- Temperature uniformity survey record
- Wiring and control diagrams
- Spare parts list with quantities and lead times
- Element or burner specifications
- Refractory material specification and service interval
- Instrument calibration certificates

**Training**

- Hands-on operation of the control system
- Normal start-up and shutdown procedure
- What to do when something is not normal
- Who to call, and how

**Parts**

- The spare parts kit agreed at order
- Wear parts sized for your current duty cycle, not for a lighter one

### For remote commissioning

Where distance makes an on-site visit impractical, commissioning can be run remotely with the customer operating the furnace and our engineers supporting by video.

It works, but it depends on two things: reliable connectivity at your site, and someone at your end who can carry out the physical steps accurately. Where either is in doubt, on-site commissioning is cheaper than a second trip.

Remote commissioning is also where the training record matters more, because there is nobody on site afterwards to catch what was missed.

### A note on the acceptance test

The uniformity survey at commissioning is the acceptance test. Everything in the specification about temperature should be verifiable against it.

If a supplier will not commit to a documented survey at commissioning, ask why. It is the single most useful document the furnace will ever produce, because it is the only way to tell in three years whether the machine is still performing.

### What to do at handover

Run the furnace on production parts yourself for a shift before the engineer leaves, if the schedule allows. You will find things a test run does not — awkward loading habits, handling bottlenecks, an interlock everyone keeps bypassing.

Those are cheap to fix during commissioning and expensive to fix afterwards.
